## Experimental Hydrogen Yield in Steam Gasification: A Comprehensive Review

### Introduction

Hydrogen-rich gas production through steam gasification of biomass and waste materials is a rapidly advancing field, driven by the global shift toward sustainable energy and the need for low-carbon hydrogen sources. The efficiency and yield of hydrogen (H₂) in such processes are critical metrics for evaluating the viability of gasification technologies. This report provides an in-depth, evidence-based analysis of experimental hydrogen yields—expressed in mol/g or mol/kg—from recent peer-reviewed studies and authoritative technical reports. The focus is on catalytic and non-catalytic steam gasification of biomass and plastic wastes, with particular attention to the effects of catalyst type, loading, and process conditions.

### Experimental Hydrogen Yields: Key Findings

#### 1. Catalytic Steam Gasification of Biomass Using Ni/Zr-MOF Catalyst

One of the most recent and methodologically robust studies is by Shang et al. (2020), who investigated the steam gasification of a biomass blend (wet sludge and wheat straw) using a nickel-supported zirconium metal-organic framework (Ni/Zr-MOF) catalyst in a fixed-bed reactor at 500 °C. The experimental setup involved 1 g each of wet sludge (80% moisture) and wheat straw, mixed and placed with 1 g of catalyst. The results are particularly noteworthy:

- **Without catalyst:** H₂ content in syngas was 1.94 vol%, with an H₂ yield of 0.39 mol/kg.
- **With Ni/Zr-MOF catalyst:** H₂ content increased to 47.49 vol%, and the H₂ yield rose dramatically to 11.87 mol/kg.
- **Catalyst stability:** After 10 reuse cycles, the H₂ yield remained high at 10.11 mol/kg, indicating excellent catalyst stability ([Shang et al., 2020](https://bioresources.cnr.ncsu.edu/resources/hydrogen-rich-syngas-production-via-catalytic-gasification-of-biomass-using-ni-zr-mof-catalyst/)).

These results demonstrate that advanced Ni-based catalysts, especially when supported on high-surface-area MOFs, can enhance hydrogen yield by over 30 times compared to non-catalytic gasification under otherwise identical conditions.

#### 2. Fe-Based Catalysts in Biomass Steam Gasification

A 2022 study published in PMC explored the use of Fe-based composite catalysts with varying iron loadings for steam gasification of biomass. The optimal Fe loading was found to be 10 wt%, yielding the following results under optimal conditions:

- **Hydrogen content:** Up to 42.2% in the product gas.
- **Hydrogen yield:** 27.65 g/kg of biomass ([PMC9039233](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9039233/)).

To convert grams to moles for hydrogen (molar mass ≈ 2 g/mol):

\[
\text{Hydrogen yield} = \frac{27.65\,g}{2\,g/mol} = 13.83\,mol/kg
\]

This yield is comparable to that achieved with advanced Ni-based catalysts, highlighting the potential of Fe-based systems for cost-effective hydrogen production.

#### 3. Ni-Based Catalysts: Loading and Performance

A comprehensive review by the Royal Society of Chemistry (2025) analyzed multiple studies on Ni-based catalysts for steam gasification of plastics and biomass. Key findings include:

- **Effect of Ni loading:** Increasing Ni loading from 0 to 10 wt% significantly boosts H₂ yield, but further increases (up to 20 wt%) do not enhance yield and may lead to catalyst deactivation due to carbon deposition ([RSC, 2025](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2025/ya/d4ya00292j)).
- **Typical yields:** In one referenced experiment, H₂ yield increased from 3.93 mmol/g (3.93 mol/kg) without catalyst to 29.35 mmol/g (29.35 mol/kg) with 10 wt% Ni catalyst. However, these high yields are often achieved under optimized laboratory conditions and may not always be replicable at scale.

#### 4. Influence of Temperature and Steam-to-Biomass Ratio

Process parameters such as temperature and steam-to-biomass (S/B) ratio are critical in determining hydrogen yield:

- **Temperature:** Raising the reactor temperature from 700 °C to 900 °C can increase H₂ yield by up to 20% ([RSC, 2025](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2025/ya/d4ya00292j)).
- **Steam-to-biomass ratio:** An S/B ratio of 1.5 has been shown to enhance both CO and H₂ yields, with water vapor increasing H₂ yield by up to 2.5 times compared to inert atmospheres.

#### 5. Non-Catalytic Gasification

Non-catalytic steam gasification typically yields much lower hydrogen concentrations and yields. For example, in the aforementioned Ni/Zr-MOF study, the H₂ yield without catalyst was only 0.39 mol/kg, and H₂ content was below 2 vol% ([Shang et al., 2020](https://bioresources.cnr.ncsu.edu/resources/hydrogen-rich-syngas-production-via-catalytic-gasification-of-biomass-using-ni-zr-mof-catalyst/)).

#### 6. Catalyst Deactivation and Regeneration

Ni-based catalysts are susceptible to deactivation via carbon deposition and sintering. Strategies to mitigate deactivation include:

- **Guard beds:** Use of dolomite guard beds to capture tars and reduce carbon deposition.
- **Dopants:** Addition of lanthanum and other rare earth elements to improve catalyst stability and reduce coking ([RSC, 2025](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2025/ya/d4ya00292j)).

### Discussion and Analysis

#### Reliability and Significance of Results

The most reliable and significant results are those from recent, peer-reviewed studies using advanced characterization and analytical techniques. The Ni/Zr-MOF study ([Shang et al., 2020](https://bioresources.cnr.ncsu.edu/resources/hydrogen-rich-syngas-production-via-catalytic-gasification-of-biomass-using-ni-zr-mof-catalyst/)) stands out for its detailed experimental design, catalyst characterization, and demonstration of catalyst stability over multiple cycles. The Fe-based catalyst study ([PMC9039233](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9039233/)) is also significant, showing that non-noble metal catalysts can achieve yields comparable to Ni-based systems.

The RSC review ([RSC, 2025](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2025/ya/d4ya00292j)) provides a broad context, confirming that optimal hydrogen yields are typically achieved with 10 wt% Ni loading and that further increases do not yield additional benefits due to deactivation risks.

#### Impact of Process Variables

- **Catalyst selection and loading** are the most critical factors in maximizing hydrogen yield. Both Ni and Fe-based catalysts, when properly supported and dispersed, can achieve yields in the range of 11–14 mol/kg under moderate temperatures.
- **Temperature** is a secondary but important factor; higher temperatures generally favor higher yields but may also increase operational costs and catalyst degradation.
- **Feedstock composition** (e.g., biomass vs. plastics, moisture content, particle size) also influences yield, but catalyst effects are dominant.

#### Practical Implications

The data suggest that, under optimized laboratory conditions, hydrogen yields from catalytic steam gasification of biomass can reach 11–14 mol/kg, with even higher yields possible for plastic-rich feedstocks. Non-catalytic processes, by contrast, yield less than 1 mol/kg under similar conditions. These findings have direct implications for the design and operation of industrial-scale hydrogen production systems, highlighting the necessity of advanced catalyst systems for economic viability.

### Conclusion

Based on the most recent and reliable experimental data, the following conclusions can be drawn:

- **Catalytic steam gasification using advanced Ni-based or Fe-based catalysts can achieve hydrogen yields in the range of 11–14 mol/kg of biomass under moderate temperatures (500–700 °C).**
- **Non-catalytic gasification yields are typically below 1 mol/kg, underscoring the critical role of catalysts.**
- **Catalyst stability and resistance to deactivation are essential for sustained high yields, with MOF-supported Ni catalysts showing particular promise.**
- **Process optimization (temperature, S/B ratio, feedstock preparation) can further enhance yields but is secondary to catalyst effects.**

Given these findings, the deployment of robust, high-surface-area catalysts—especially Ni/Zr-MOF and Fe-composite systems—should be prioritized in future hydrogen production technologies based on steam gasification.
